Divvy With Stats

Author

Ian Campbell

TLDR

What if you used statistics to handle large datasets? Sample size doesn’t depend on the size of the overall dataset, so when the dataset is in the gigabytes, you can get very sensitive results from a small sample that you can run locally on your machine.

Divvy Case Study

Back in 2024, I did a case study on Divvy bike sharing data. Divvy is a bikeshare program in Chicago that publishes anonymized ridership data. I analyzed a year of their data using R tidyverse. There were about 1.2 gigs of data; small enough to fit in memory. I wanted to revisit the Divvy dataset and ask a different set of questions that involved a larger portion of the data. In 2024, I asked about differences between member and casual riders, to guide marketing strategy. What if I asked about differences between pre- and post-Covid ridership, as this might guide changes in policy that were set in pre-covid era? Are there more or less riders now than pre-covid? Has the mix of ridership types changed? Were seasonality trends–hourly, weekly, monthly– consistent year over year? Did the geographic distribution change?

Asking questions over the past 10 years would use about 12 gigs of memory, surpassing the limit on my machine, and making queries run much slower. This challenge would occur with historical data in lots of settings, and I wanted a general purpose strategy to handle it.

Using a framework like Spark is general purpose and likely sufficient for this problem, but, having gained some understanding of statistics, I wondered how far a sampling strategy could go.

Sampling

To set an upper bound on the sample size, my computer can manage 2 gigs in memory, and slow down a manageable amount.

The first question is, what should my sample size be? How do I convert my questions from before into statistical measures?

Most of the measures in my questions can be expressd as proportions. For a question like, “did the daily/monthly/weekly pattern of use change between pre- and post-covid?” we need a threshold for what counts as meaningful change. For the weekly breakdown, which has the smallest meaningful difference observed in my 2024 analysis, the difference between proportions needed to be at least 5% to recognize a weekend trend. It would seem that our margin of error should be, at most, 2.5%. However, we will be making multiple comparisons: pre-covid vs post-covid, member vs casual, and weekday vs weekend. 2.5% error could compound to something larger than 5%, so we have to be more conservative. An error margin of 1% should preserve a 5% difference. Different expected proportions yield different sample sizes, but we’ll start by using the most conservative expected proportion.

Confidence level

I ran into a hiccup; When you do lots of measures, the likelihood of getting a type 1 error increases. The equation looks like prob_type_1_error = 1-(1-alpha)^n_comparisons. For example,

  • n_comparisons = 10: 1 - (0.95)^{10} ≈ 0.401
  • n_comparisons = 100: 1 - (0.95)^{100} ≈ 0.994

There are several solutions for this, but a popular (and conservative) one is the Bonferroni correction. This correction ensures that the family-wise error rate (the error rate for all comparisons together) is less than our given confidence level, .05.

My proportions are rendered as bar graphs, where we compare each bar to each of the other bars, treating each comparison as a hypothesis test. This is extremely conservative, with numBars**2/2 claimed comparisons, but as we’ll see, our decision isn’t sensitive to the resulting increase in sample size. We will estimate a few groups. First, there’s the yearly breakdown, let’s say 10 years. Then there’s the member vs casual comparison, 2 groups. Then there’s the day of week comparison, 7 groups. So we have 10*2*7 = 140 comparisons. We do the same for the monthly breakdown, 12*2*7 = 168 comparisons. We do the same for the hour of day breakdown, 24*2*7 = 336 comparisons.= 80,360 comparisons. For the geographical distribution, I render station amounts as a heatmap. We might check each year, and treat each station as a proportion estimate. So 4 years * ~4000 stations = 16000. Again, treating a heatmap as a collection of individual hypothesis tests is wildly cautious.

Modifying n comparisons doesn’t cause as large a change in sample size as modifying margin error: when n comparisons is already large, doubling it only adds a few thousand to the sample size. If samples depended on something expensive like field-work or surveys, we would be sensitive to the change, and we’d consider something less stringent, like controlling for the false discovery rate instead of the family-wise error rate, or specifying more precise hypotheses. But here, those considerations are moot: 10,000 more samples means a negligible increase in compute time.

Let’s run the numbers, using the sample size formula for proportions. 

nBarComparisons = (140^2 + 168^2 + 336^2)/2
nStations = 16000
n_comparisons = nStations + nBarComparisons

alpha_familywise = .05
alpha_per = alpha_familywise/n_comparisons

z = qnorm(1-alpha_per/2)
margin_error = .01
expectedProportion = .5
sampleSize = (z^2 * expectedProportion * (1-expectedProportion)) / margin_error^2
sampleSize = round(sampleSize)
sampleSize
[1] 62981

For a sanity check, let’s make sure we have enough power to detect the smallest meaningful difference. For the proportion power test, we need a null and alternative hypothesis, p2 and p1. In our case, we aren’t testing specific hypothesis, we just want a conservative estimate. Proportion estimates and comparisons require the biggest sample size when they are close to 50%. We’ll set that as our null hypothesis. As we said before, our target effect size is ultimately about 1%, so we’ll subtract .01 from .5 to get the other proportion. We also divide the significance level by n_comparisons, which is how we apply the Bonferroni correction. Finally, The resulting sample size needs to be doubled, since it describes the sample size for each individual, rather than the combined, proportions being tested. 

power.prop.test(p1 = c(.45, .475, .485, .49), p2 = .5, n = max(sampleSize), sig.level = .05/n_comparisons)

Our power drops off fast: at an effect-size of .025, it’s ~1, but at an effect-size of .01, it’s ~0.13, which is unacceptably low.

Let’s see a sample size that would yield a power of .8.

sampleSize = power.prop.test(p1 = .49, p2 = .5, power = c(.8), sig.level = .05/n_comparisons)$n *2
sampleSize
[1] 343452.3

Using Bonferroni correction, and adjusting for a power of .8, our max required sample size is 343452.3. This is a pittance, ~45 MB of data, and orders of magnitude less than the 2 gb I could handle. I could increase this value 20-fold, erasing any doubt about the accuracy of the results, and still be well under 2 gb. Moreover, this sample size doesn’t grow with the size of the population; once the population is large, sample size only depends on our required precision and confidence, not on the size of the population. So we can handle even larger datasets using this technique.

Man, statistics are powerful!

Distributing the sample size across buckets

We can’t load all of our data into memory at once, but we can load it in chunks. So we need to distribute the sample size across buckets.

Now that we have a sample size, a naive strategy (suggested by AI) is to distribute the samples equally between each month long bucket. However, this over-represents months with low ridership, and under-represents months with high ridership.

Better would be to sample a percentage of each bucket. sampleSize_bucket = nrow(bucketDF)\*minPercent. minPercent = sampleSize_total / totalPopSize converts the sample size into a percentage.

We don’t know the exact population size, but we know that there are about 12 gigs of data. To be conservative, we want our sample size to be a larger percentage of the population: samp_size/popSize; a smaller popSize gives a larger percentage. popSize = allDataSize/rowSize so overestimating the size of a row gives a smaller population estimate. Each row has 13 columns, and most are strings, say 5-10 chars. So we can estimate that each row is about 10*13 = 130 bytes. So we have about 12* 1024^3 / 130, or approximately 100 million rows.

We should note that doubling nChars doubles the amount of data we ostensibly need. 

nchars = 10
ncols = 13
ngigs = 12
popSize = (ngigs * 1024^3) / (nchars*ncols)

proportion = sampleSize[1] / popSize 
proportion
[1] 0.003465202

how many MB is our ultimate sampleSize?

sampleSize * nchars*ncols / 1024^2
[1] 42.58041

Now we can write code that takes each bucket or csv file, samples a percentage, and writes the aggregated samples to a new file. 

fileNames = list.files("~/Developer/DataAnalytics/divvy/bigData/", full.names = TRUE)


iterativeSample = function(fileNames, outputFileName, proportion){
  allMonths = lapply(fileNames, function(fileName) {
    df = read.csv(fileName)
    sampleSize = ceiling(nrow(df) * proportion)
    sampled = df[sample(1:nrow(df), sampleSize), ]
    sampled
  })
  allMonths = do.call(rbind, allMonths)
  write_csv(allMonths, outputFileName)
}

Implementation in Other Projects

The point of this case study is to save time. However, as described, the technique is not plug and play; you need to be somewhat conversant in statistics, and you have to do some not so back-of-the-envelope calculations to get the sample size right. It’s tempting to create a rule of thumb; a sample size of 400,000 should be conservative for 1% margin of error, and up to 100,000 comparisons, regardless of the population size, once the population is large. However, this is unintuitive: how can such a small proportion of the overall population yield reasonable results? How will you explain this to your boss, who may have no background in statistics? Using formulas straight from a reputable source like a textbook or base R package lends credibility.

Areas where sampling wouldn’t be recommended: when your stakeholders need financial precision, when you’re looking for rare events, when you’re looking for absolute sums and not proportions, or when a single observation is spread across multiple tables, like with user website behavior.

Calculations for other statistical measures

What if we were handling averages?

For averages, there’s a different formula. You need to estimate the standard deviation. 

# Bonferroni correction
alpha_familywise = .05
alpha_per = alpha_familywise/n_comparisons

sigma_vals = c(1, 10)
E_vals = c(2, 1, 0.1, 0.01)
           # Margin of error
z = qnorm(1-alpha_per/2) 

sigma_e_grid = expand.grid(E = E_vals, sigma = sigma_vals)
sigma_e_grid$ratio =  sigma_e_grid$sigma / sigma_e_grid$E
sigma_e_grid$n_required = ceiling((z^2 * sigma_e_grid$sigma^2) / sigma_e_grid$E^2)
sigma_e_grid$approx_mb =  sigma_e_grid$n_required *13*10/1024^2

With averages, (and with proportions) sample size is very sensitive to margin of error. Sample size for averages is similarly sensitive to std_dev. So the sample size is dominated by the ratio of margin of error to std_dev. Given that these values depend largely on the data domain, calculation-saving rules of thumb are hard to create: a small change in the ratio results in a huge difference in the sample size. You need to calculate sample size to be safe.

Though I won’t give a rule of thumb, there’s reason to expect that when estimating averages, you’ll still get a small required sample size relative to your population size. As with proportions, the sample size doesn’t grow with the size of the population; it depends entirely on the ratio of margin of error to std_dev, and the precision you need. As long as the standard deviation is not enormous relative to the effect size you care about, say, E is 1% of sigma, the math points to hundreds of thousands of rows—not millions—as enough.

Conclusion

It’s orders of magnitude more efficient to analyze a sample of a large dataset, rather than analyze it in its entirety. Rather than burdening your computer with gigabytes of data, you can use statistics to analyze a small sample, and still be confident in your results.
